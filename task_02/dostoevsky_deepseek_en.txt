In setting about the description of the recent and so strange events that occurred in our town, hitherto distinguished by nothing, I am compelled, through my ineptitude, to begin somewhat from afar, namely with certain biographical details about the talented and much-esteemed Stepan Trofimovich Verkhovensky. Let these details serve merely as an introduction to the chronicle I propose, while the story itself that I intend to describe is still to come.

I will say directly: Stepan Trofimovich constantly played among us a certain special and, so to speak, civic role, and he loved that role to passion—so much so that, it seems to me, he could not have lived without it. Not that I would equate him with an actor on the stage: God forbid, especially since I myself respect him. All this may have been a matter of habit, or, better to say, of a ceaseless and noble inclination, from childhood, toward the pleasant dream of a beautiful civic presentation of himself. He, for example, was exceedingly fond of his position as a “persecuted” man and, so to speak, an “exile.” In those two little words there is a sort of classical brilliance that seduced him once and for all, and, elevating him gradually in his own opinion over so many years, finally brought him to a certain very lofty and self-love-flattering pedestal. In a satirical English novel of the last century, a certain Gulliver, returning from the land of the Lilliputians, where people were only some two inches tall, became so accustomed to considering himself a giant among them that, even walking the streets of London, he involuntarily shouted to passers-by and carriages to turn aside before him and beware lest he somehow crush them, imagining that he was still a giant and they were little. For this they laughed at him and scolded him, and rude coachmen even lashed the giant with their whips; but was that just? What cannot habit do? Habit led Stepan Trofimovich almost to the same thing, but in an even more innocent and harmless form, if one may put it so, because he was a most excellent man.

I even think that toward the end everyone and everywhere had forgotten him; but one cannot possibly say that he had not been known at all before. It is indisputable that for a time he too belonged to the famous pleiad of certain celebrated figures of our past generation, and for a time—though only for the tiniest little moment—his name was uttered by many hurried people of that day almost alongside the names of Chaadaev, Belinsky, Granovsky, and Herzen, who was only then beginning abroad. But Stepan Trofimovich’s activity ended almost at the same moment it began—so to speak, from a “whirlwind of converging circumstances.” And what then? Not only was there no “whirlwind,” but even no “circumstances” at all afterward, at least in this case. Only now, the other day, I learned, to my greatest surprise, but with complete certainty, that Stepan Trofimovich lived among us, in our province, not only not in exile, as we had been accustomed to think, but had never even been under surveillance. Such, then, is the power of one’s own imagination! He sincerely believed all his life that in certain spheres he was constantly feared, that his steps were continually known and counted, and that each of the three governors who had succeeded one another among us in the last twenty years, upon entering to rule the province, had already brought with him a certain special and troublesome thought about him, suggested from above and, above all, upon the handing over of the province. If someone had then convinced the most honest Stepan Trofimovich with irrefutable proofs that he had nothing whatever to fear, he would certainly have been offended. And yet he was a man of the greatest intelligence and talent, a man, so to speak, even of science, though, however, in science... well, in a word, in science he had done not so very much and, it seems, nothing at all. But with men of science in our Russia that happens constantly.

He returned from abroad and shone as a lecturer from the university chair at the very end of the forties. He managed to deliver only a few lectures, and, it seems, on the Arabians; he also managed to defend a brilliant dissertation on the civil and Hanseatic significance that had been arising in the German town of Hanau, in the epoch between 1413 and 1428, and along with it on those special and unclear reasons why this significance never came to be. This dissertation cleverly and painfully stung the Slavophiles of that day and at once procured him numerous and furious enemies among them. Afterwards—however, after the loss of his chair—he managed to publish (so to speak, as retaliation and to show whom they had lost) in a monthly and progressive journal that translated from Dickens and preached George Sand, the beginning of one of the most profound investigations—it seems, on the causes of the extraordinary moral nobility of certain knights in a certain epoch, or something of the sort. At least some higher and extraordinarily noble idea was conveyed. They said afterwards that the continuation of the investigation was hastily prohibited and that even the progressive journal suffered for the published first half. It may very well have been so, because what was there not then? But in the present case it is more probable that there was nothing, and that the author himself was too lazy to finish the investigation. He stopped his lectures on the Arabians because a letter to someone with an exposition of certain “circumstances” was somehow intercepted by someone (obviously by his retrograde enemies), as a result of which someone demanded certain explanations from him. I do not know whether it is true, but they asserted also that at the very same time in Petersburg a certain enormous, unnatural, and anti-state society was discovered, consisting of thirteen people, and almost shook the building. They said that they supposedly intended to translate Fourier himself. As if on purpose, at the very same time in Moscow Stepan Trofimovich’s poem was seized, written by him some six years before, in Berlin, in his very first youth, and circulating in manuscript copies between two lovers and one student. This poem now lies in my desk too; I received it no later than last year, in a manuscript copy in his own hand, quite recent, from Stepan Trofimovich himself, with his inscription and in a magnificent red morocco binding. However, it is not without poetry and even not without a certain talent; strange, but then (that is, more correctly, in the thirties) people often wrote in that style. I find it difficult to relate the plot, for, in truth, I understand nothing in it. It is some sort of allegory, in lyric-dramatic form and reminiscent of the second part of Faust. The scene opens with a chorus of women, then a chorus of men, then of certain forces, and at the end of it all a chorus of souls that have not yet lived but would very much like to live. All these choruses sing about something very indefinite, mostly about someone’s curse, but with a shade of higher humor. But the scene suddenly changes, and a sort of “Feast of Life” begins, at which even insects sing, a tortoise appears with certain Latin sacramental words, and even, if I remember, a mineral sang about something, that is, an object entirely inanimate. In general, however, everyone sings incessantly, and if they talk, they somehow vaguely scold, but again with a shade of higher significance. Finally, the scene changes again, and a wild place appears, and among the cliffs wanders a civilized young man, who plucks and sucks certain herbs, and to the fairy’s question: why does he suck these herbs?—he answers that, feeling an excess of life in himself, he seeks oblivion and finds it in the juice of these herbs; but that his chief desire is to lose his mind as quickly as possible (a desire perhaps superfluous). Then suddenly a youth of indescribable beauty rides in on a black horse, and after him follows a terrible multitude of all peoples. The youth represents Death, and all peoples thirst for her. And finally, in the very last scene, the Tower of Babel suddenly appears, and certain athletes finally complete it with a song of new hope, and when they have completed it to the very top, the owner, let us say even of Olympus, runs away in comic fashion, and humanity, having guessed, seizes his place and immediately begins a new life with a new perception of things. Well, this poem was then found dangerous. Last year I suggested to Stepan Trofimovich that he publish it, in view of its complete innocence in our time, but he declined the suggestion with visible displeasure. The opinion of its complete innocence did not please him, and I even attribute to that a certain coldness on his part toward me, which lasted a whole two months. And what then? Suddenly, almost at the same time as I suggested publishing it here, our poem is published there, that is, abroad, in one of the revolutionary miscellanies, and completely without Stepan Trofimovich’s knowledge. He was frightened at first, rushed to the governor, and wrote a most noble letter of justification to Petersburg, read it to me twice, but did not send it, not knowing to whom to address it. In a word, he was agitated for a whole month; but I am convinced that in the mysterious recesses of his heart he was extraordinarily flattered. He almost slept with the copy of the miscellany delivered to him, and by day hid it under his mattress and would not even let the woman make the bed, and although he expected every day some telegram from somewhere, he looked down on everyone. No telegram came. Then he also made peace with me, which testifies to the extraordinary goodness of his quiet and unforgetful heart.

I do not assert that he suffered not at all; I have only now become fully convinced that he could have continued about his Arabians as much as he liked, provided he gave the necessary explanations. But at that time he tormented himself and with particular haste arranged to convince himself once and for all that his career had been shattered for his whole life by a “whirlwind of circumstances.” And if the whole truth is to be told, the real reason for the change in his career was the earlier and again renewed most delicate proposal to him from Varvara Petrovna Stavrogina, the wife of a lieutenant general and a substantial rich woman, to take upon himself the education and all the mental development of her only son, as supreme tutor and friend, not to mention the brilliant remuneration. This proposal had been made to him for the first time back in Berlin, and precisely at the very time when he first became a widower. His first wife was a frivolous young lady from our province, whom he married in his very first and still reckless youth, and, it seems, he endured much grief with this, by the way attractive, person, for lack of means to support her and, moreover, for other, partly already delicate reasons. She died in Paris, having been separated from him for the last three years, and leaving him a five-year-old son, “the fruit of first, joyful, and as yet unclouded love,” as once escaped from the grieving Stepan Trofimovich in my presence. The nestling was sent to Russia from the very beginning, where he was brought up all the time in the hands of certain distant aunts, somewhere in the backwoods. Stepan Trofimovich rejected Varvara Petrovna’s proposal at that time and quickly married again, even before a year had passed, to a taciturn little Berlin German, and, mainly, without any particular need. But besides this, there turned out to be other reasons for refusing the post of tutor: he was tempted by the then-resounding fame of one unforgettable professor, and he, in turn, flew to the chair for which he had prepared, to try out his eagle wings as well. And now, with wings already singed, he naturally remembered the proposal that had even before wavered his decision. The sudden death of his second wife, who had not lived with him even a year, settled everything finally. I will say directly: everything was resolved by the ardent participation and precious, so to speak classical, friendship for him of Varvara Petrovna, if only one can express oneself that way about friendship. He threw himself into the embraces of this friendship, and the affair was cemented for more than twenty years. I used the expression “threw himself into the embraces,” but God forbid anyone should think anything superfluous and idle; these embraces must be understood in one only most highly moral sense. The subtlest and most delicate bond united these two so remarkable beings forever.

The post of tutor was accepted also because the small estate left after Stepan Trofimovich’s first wife—a very small one—lay quite next to Skvoreshniki, the magnificent suburban estate of the Stavrogins in our province. Moreover, it was always possible, in the quiet of the study and no longer distracted by the vastness of university duties, to devote oneself to the cause of science and enrich the national literature with the most profound investigations. Investigations did not appear; but in return it became possible to stand for the whole rest of his life, more than twenty years, so to speak, as an “incarnate reproach” before the fatherland, in the words of the people’s poet:

As an incarnate reproach  
You stood before the fatherland,  
Liberal-idealist.

But that person of whom the people’s poet spoke might perhaps have had the right to pose in this sense all his life, if he had wished to, though it is boring. Our Stepan Trofimovich, in truth, was only an imitator compared with such persons, and he also grew tired of standing and often lay on his side. But even though on his side, the incarnateness of reproach was preserved even in a lying position—justice must be done, especially since for the province even that was sufficient. You should have looked at him in our club when he sat down to cards. His whole appearance said: “Cards! I am sitting down with you to play eralash! Is that compatible? Who is answerable for this? Who shattered my activity and turned it into eralash? Eh, perish Russia!”—and he imposingly trumped with hearts.

And in truth, he terribly loved to do battle at cards, for which, and especially in the last time, he had frequent and unpleasant clashes with Varvara Petrovna, all the more because he constantly lost. But of that later. I will note only that he was a man even conscientious (that is, sometimes), and therefore often sad. Throughout the whole twenty-year friendship with Varvara Petrovna, he regularly fell three or four times a year into what we called between ourselves “civic grief,” that is, simply into the blues, but the little word pleased the much-respected Varvara Petrovna. Later, in addition to civic grief, he also began to fall into champagne; but the sensitive Varvara Petrovna protected him all his life from all trivial inclinations. And indeed he needed a nurse, because he sometimes became very strange: in the midst of the most exalted grief he would suddenly begin to laugh in the most common-people’s manner. There were moments when he even began to speak of himself in a humorous sense. But Varvara Petrovna feared nothing so much as a humorous sense. This was a woman-classic, a woman-patroness, acting with only the highest considerations in view. The twenty-year influence of this exalted lady on her poor friend was capital. About her one ought to speak separately, which I shall do.
